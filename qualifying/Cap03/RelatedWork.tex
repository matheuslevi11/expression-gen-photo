The systems reviewed in this chapter share one goal with the present work: producing a
photorealistic face whose expression is directly specified. They differ in what
they accept as that specification and in what they require as a starting point, and those two
choices divide them into three task formulations. Expression \emph{transfer} copies the
expression of a reference image onto a target identity, so the specification is itself an image. Expression \emph{editing} modifies a given source image, so the
specification is a change relative to whatever expression that source already displays. Expression-conditioned \emph{generation} synthesises a face from text under a numeric action-unit condition, with no source image at all.

The chapter is organised by these formulations, because what
separates them is also what determines whether a system can meet the requirements set out in
Section~\ref{sec:problem}. Only the third admits a numeric intensity as input without a reference
image, which is why FineFace \citep{varanka2024fineface} serves as the reference point for the
head-to-head comparison of Chapter~\ref{cap:preliminary-results}.
Section~\ref{sec:rw-positioning} returns to the comparison once the three formulations have been
examined.

\section{Expression Transfer from an Exemplar}\label{sec:rw-transfer}

% \citet{jiang2024emojidiff}: control signal is an RGB exemplar image
% (masked face, CLIP-encoded) injected via IP-Adapter-style decoupled
% cross-attention into frozen SD1.5; identity from a reference photo;
% ArcFace identity loss on one-step decodes.
% Relation: adjacent, not competing. It cannot accept "smile = 0.5" as
% input; a user must supply an exemplar per intensity level.
% Their Sec. 1 critique of signal-conditioned methods (detail loss,
% dependence on third-party detector accuracy) applies to this work and
% should be acknowledged and answered in Cap04.

EmojiDiff \citep{jiang2024emojidiff} performs expression transfer with strong identity
preservation. Its control signal is an RGB \emph{exemplar}: a masked face region is CLIP-encoded
and injected into a frozen Stable Diffusion~1.5 through IP-Adapter-style decoupled
cross-attention \citep{ye2023ipadapter}, while identity is carried by a separate reference photo,
and an ArcFace identity loss \citep{deng2019arcface} is applied to one-step latent decodes. The
training data is manufactured: a base adaptor generates roughly 100k cross-identity expression
triplets by face-swapping about 10k in-house images.

EmojiDiff is adjacent to this work in the sense that its interface cannot accept a
numeric command such as ``smile $=0.5$'': a user must supply an exemplar image for every
intensity level, and there is no notion of an ordered trajectory or of a sequence generated as
one clip. It does, however, raise the standard objection to signal-conditioned methods -- loss
of fine detail and dependence on a third-party detector's accuracy -- which applies to this work
and is answered by the independent-detector evaluation of Section~\ref{sec:method-evaluation}.

\section{Expression Editing of a Source Image}\label{sec:rw-editing}

\subsection{Action-Unit Conditioned Editing}\label{subsec:rw-magicface}

% \citet{wei2025magicface}: 12-dim AU *variation* vector (target minus
% source), injected through a linear layer added to the time embedding;
% FULL fine-tune of the denoising UNet plus a UNet-copy ID encoder;
% AU dropout plus CFG on the AU condition.
% Relation: closest in control semantics (continuous AU intensity on SD),
% but editing rather than generation, relative rather than absolute targets
% (requires source-AU estimation at inference), no temporal mechanism, and
% far more trainable parameters.
% Their lab-vs-wild ablation predicts this work's lab-domain artifacts.

MagicFace \citep{wei2025magicface} is the closest system in control \emph{semantics}: it
conditions on a continuous twelve-dimensional action-unit vector on a Stable Diffusion backbone.
Two design choices set it apart from this work. First, its condition is a \emph{relative} AU
\emph{variation} (target minus source), injected through a single linear layer added to the time
embedding, so the target expression is specified as a change from the source; at inference this
requires estimating the source image's action units first. Second, it \emph{fully} fine-tunes
the denoising UNet and adds a UNet-sized copy as an identity encoder -- an order of magnitude
more trainable parameters than the adaptor of Chapter~\ref{cap:method} -- trained on tens of
thousands of same-identity pairs. It edits a given image instead of generating it from text, and
each edited image is independent, with no temporal mechanism. Its own lab-versus-wild ablation,
in which a model trained on lab-recorded faces fails to generalise to natural images.

\subsection{Intensity as a Text-Embedding Direction}\label{subsec:rw-pixelsmile}

% \citet{hua2026pixelsmile}: intensity alpha scales a text-embedding
% direction on Qwen-Image-Edit with LoRA; source of the CLS protocol
% adopted in Cap04.
% Two points must be made:
%   1. Their zero-shot row (no training, text-embedding interpolation alone)
%      already achieves substantial linearity. This is the "why not just
%      prompt it?" objection and it must be answered, not ignored -- hence
%      the prompt-engineering baseline planned in Cap06.
%   2. Their negative MEAD result does NOT transfer: they mapped MEAD's
%      three discrete levels to {0.5, 0.75, 1.0}, discarding the continuous
%      per-frame signal this work extracts.

PixelSmile \citep{hua2026pixelsmile} is the closest system in \emph{claim}: continuous, linear
intensity control evaluated as a correlation between the commanded intensity and a detected one, which is the protocol this work adopts for calibration. It scales
a text-embedding direction by a scalar $\alpha$ on an image-editing backbone with LoRA, and
reports a correlation of $0.81$. Two points follow. First, its zero-shot row, a text-embedding
interpolation with \emph{no} training, already reaches $0.69$ on the same score, which gives a baseline for a prompt-only result. Second, its negative
result on \gls{mead} does not transfer to this work: PixelSmile mapped \gls{mead}'s three
discrete intensity levels onto $\{0.5,0.75,1.0\}$, discarding the continuous per-frame signal
that Chapter~\ref{cap:method} extracts, so the comparison is not in equal terms. As with MagicFace, PixelSmile edits a source image, its levels are independent stills, and its $\alpha$
is unitless.

\section{Action-Unit Conditioned Generation}\label{sec:rw-fineface}

% THE PRIOR ART. State this plainly and early: \citet{varanka2024fineface}
% (July 2024) generates faces from text conditioned on a continuous 12-dim
% AU intensity vector, with no source image, using an adapter on a frozen
% backbone plus CFG on the AU condition, and demonstrates out-of-range
% extrapolation. Its control is BROADER than this work's single AU12 axis.
% Any claim of primacy for "AU-conditioned generation" is therefore
% unavailable and must not appear anywhere in the text.
%
% What it does not do, and this is the opening this work occupies:
%   - its intensity sweep is N INDEPENDENT stills, sharing only a seed;
%     nothing binds identity across levels architecturally;
%   - it reports NO face-identity metric at all;
%   - intensity control is shown qualitatively only, with the authors
%     conceding the scale is nonlinear; there is no commanded-vs-detected
%     correlation anywhere.

FineFace \citep{varanka2024fineface} is the prior art for this work's task, and is stated as
such plainly. It generates a face from text alone, with no source image, conditioned on a
continuous twelve-dimensional action-unit vector, using an adapter and a rank-32 LoRA on a
frozen backbone with classifier-free guidance on the action-unit condition, and it demonstrates
out-of-range extrapolation of the intensity scale. Its control is \emph{broader} than the single
AU12 axis of Chapter~\ref{cap:introduction}: twelve action units and their combinations.

What FineFace does not do is precisely the opening this work occupies. Its intensity sweep is a
set of $N$ \emph{independent} stills that share only a random seed; nothing binds identity across
the levels architecturally. It reports no face-identity metric at all. And its intensity control
is shown only qualitatively, the authors themselves conceding that the scale is nonlinear; no
commanded-versus-detected correlation is reported anywhere. This work keeps FineFace's text-only,
adapter-on-frozen-backbone setting but replaces the independent stills with a single
identity-consistent clip and adds the measurement the prior art omits.
