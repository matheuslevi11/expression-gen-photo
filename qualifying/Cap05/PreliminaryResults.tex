This chapter reports what has been measured on the trained model to date. Each section
isolates one property with its own instrument: trajectory following, identity consistency across the trajectory, absolute calibration, the emergence of the capability over training, a head-to-head against FineFace, and the whole-face response to the single axis.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:res-setup}

% Checkpoint (100k steps, single RTX 3090), prompt bank size, seeds,
% frames per clip, intensity lists used. Point to the batch-eval harness so
% the numbers are reproducible.

The results of this section come from the released 100k-step checkpoint
(Section~\ref{sec:method-training}), generated on a single RTX~3090. Unless a table states
otherwise, evaluation uses the bank of 40 text prompts, three seeds ($42,43,44$), and
five-frame clips at $256\times384$, decoded at 25 denoising steps with classifier-free
guidance scale $8.0$. The ascending ramp commands the intensity list $(0,0.25,0.5,0.75,1.0)$;
the constant-list and permuted-list designs of Section~\ref{sec:method-evaluation} supply the
calibration and non-monotonic probes.

\section{Trajectory Following}\label{sec:res-ramp}

% Headline: control over the trajectory is real and robust at scale, and it
% is confirmed by an estimator that produced none of the training labels.
% The descending-ramp reversal near -1 is strong evidence that the model
% responds to the conditioning signal rather than to a fixed temporal prior.

Table~\ref{tab:res-ramp} establishes that trajectory following is acquired during training and robust at scale.
On the ascending ramp the trained adaptor reaches $r=0.774\pm0.212$ (median $0.86$, with
$87\%$ of clips above $0.5$), against a frozen-backbone baseline indistinguishable from zero.
The result is corroborated by the MediaPipe proxy ($r=0.829$), an independent estimator with respect to the training labels, indicating that the effect is not an artifact of the py-feat pipeline. The descending-ramp reversal to $r=-0.98$ shows the model tracks the commanded signal as expected. The permuted-list drop to $r=0.385\pm0.505$ indicates that the conditioning is genuinely per-frame, but the temporal prior resists non-monotonic trajectories, for which the ramp-only training data offers no support.

\section{Identity Consistency}\label{sec:res-identity}

% The measurement that isolates the sequence-native claim. Note the
% qualitative asymmetry that matters as much as the number: the baseline's
% faces were undetectable in 57% of samples, so its 0.85 is computed on the
% easier remainder and flatters it.

Table~\ref{tab:res-identity} shows the results for identity consistency. Within a generated clip
the trained adaptor holds a frame-to-frame identity cosine of $0.919\pm0.054$ and, more tellingly,
an adjacent-frame minimum of $0.877\pm0.086$ against the baseline's $0.794\pm0.152$; the
adjacent-frame minimum is reported because the worst transition is the one a viewer notices, thus being a very important metric for quality perception.

Two asymmetries qualify these numbers, and both run in the same direction. The baseline's faces
were undetectable in $57\%$ of samples, so its cosines are computed on the easier detectable
remainder and are flattered by that selection, which makes the gap reported here a lower bound on
the true difference. The cross-system comparison of Section~\ref{sec:res-headtohead} is
constrained in the opposite way to what might be expected: the frames measured here are
$256\times384$ video frames carrying real head motion and eye blinks, whereas the independent
stills generated by the prior art are cleaner $512\times512$ portraits, on which face embeddings
are generally more stable, so the advantage measured there is understated.
Identity is measured with a single embedding network (facenet-VGGFace2); multi-model averaging
with an ArcFace embedding is scheduled as proposed work in Chapter~\ref{cap:schedule}.

\section{Absolute Calibration}\label{sec:res-calibration}

% NEGATIVE RESULT. Report it plainly and diagnose it.
%
% Finding: holding the commanded value constant across a clip and varying it
% between clips produces almost no change in detected intensity
% (\gls{cls} = 0.07; detected AU12 flat near 0.85 for every commanded level
% at or above 0.1). The same commanded value of 0.0 renders as 0.54 inside a
% ramp and 0.79 in a constant clip.
%
% Consequence: the control is RELATIVE and contextual, not absolute. The
% conditioning commands a trajectory, not a value.
%
% Root cause: the training clips are onset-to-apex ramps only
% (Section~\ref{sec:method-data}), so the model never observed a constant
% intensity trajectory.
%
% This refutes H3 from Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses}. Say so in those words.

Holding the commanded value constant across a clip and varying it between clips over
$\{0.0, 0.1, \dots, 1.0\}$ produces almost no change in detected intensity. Over 10 prompts and
three seeds at eleven commanded levels ($n=330$), the \gls{cls} is $0.07$ and the detected AU12
remains flat near $0.85$ at every commanded level at or above $0.1$. The same commanded value
also renders differently according to its surroundings: a commanded $0.0$ is detected at $0.54$
as the first frame of an ascending ramp and at $0.79$ in a clip held constant at zero.

The control is therefore relative to the commanded trajectory instead of absolute, which refutes
hypothesis H3 of Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses}. The training data accounts for the result. The
clips are onset-to-apex ramps only, so a constant-intensity
trajectory falls outside the training distribution in precisely the dimension this protocol
probes, and no training instance exhibits the fixed commanded-to-rendered mapping that H3
presumes. The level at which the response saturates has a second source in the same data, since
\gls{mead} happy clips carry substantial AU12 even at their lowest labelled level, which places
the floor of the range near the plateau observed here.

Three remedies follow from that diagnosis and are scheduled in Chapter~\ref{cap:schedule}:
augmenting the training set with constant- and permuted-intensity clips, \gls{au} dropout with
\gls{cfg} on the expression condition \citep{wei2025magicface}, and label-distribution smoothing
\citep{varanka2024fineface}.

\section{Effect of Training Duration}\label{sec:res-dose}

The ascending-ramp evaluation of Section~\ref{sec:res-ramp} was repeated at six checkpoints
spanning the training run, with prompts, seeds and protocol held fixed so that
the checkpoint is the only variable, see Figure~\ref{fig:dose}. Against the untrained baseline of
$r=-0.05$, control reaches $r=0.55$ by 1k steps and a plateau near $0.85$ by 25k--50k. The
association with training steps is positive but not strictly monotone. Per-prompt variance shrinks as training proceeds, the standard deviation
falling from $0.43$ to $0.13$, so later training makes the control more consistent after its mean
has saturated. Trajectory following is therefore produced by the trained adaptor rather than
inherited from the frozen backbone.

\section{Head-to-Head Comparison}\label{sec:res-headtohead}

% Lab note 2026-07-21. The verdict is mixed and must be presented as mixed:
% ramp following is a TIE, FineFace WINS calibration, this work wins identity.

Table~\ref{tab:res-headtohead} reports the comparison between our method and FineFace. Trajectory
following is statistically tied: FineFace leads by $0.012$ on py-feat, well within the spread of either
system, and by a wider margin on the label-independent MediaPipe proxy, where it is ahead on
$70\%$ of prompts. Absolute calibration goes to FineFace decisively, $0.50$ against $0.07$. That
outcome is consistent with the diagnosis of Section~\ref{sec:res-calibration}, since a set of
independently generated stills provides no surrounding trajectory for a commanded value to be
read relative to, and each still must therefore carry the commanded intensity on its own.
Cross-frame identity is where our model stands out, modestly but consistently: the adjacent-frame minimum is
higher on $63$--$68\%$ of prompts with roughly $2.5\times$ lower variance.

Both outcomes follow from the same design decision. Binding the intensity levels into one
generated sequence is what supplies the cross-frame identity, since the levels share a single
denoising pass and the temporal attention that spans it. That same decision fixes the training
distribution: sequence-native training requires sequences, the sequences available in \gls{mead}
are ramps, and no ramp exhibits the fixed commanded-to-rendered mapping on which absolute
calibration depends, whereas a model trained on independent
stills carrying absolute targets is under no such constraint. The two results are therefore not
separate findings but consequences of one choice, and whether they can be decoupled, so that a
sequence-native model also calibrates absolutely, is what the training-set augmentation proposed
in Chapter~\ref{cap:schedule} is intended to establish.

\section{Whole-Face Response}\label{sec:res-coactivation}

A single scalar axis invites the question of what \emph{else} it moves. Re-scoring the
ascending-ramp clips for all twenty py-feat action units, cross-checked against an
independent estimator and against face-mesh geometry, shows that the axis drives
a coherent smile expression, see Figure~\ref{fig:coactivation}.
The commanded AU12 and the Duchenne cheek raiser AU6 co-activate strongly and are
model-induced; mouth-opening units like AU25 and AU26, rise while mouth-closing and frowning units (AU23,
AU17, AU15) fall. Brow units sit near zero on py-feat, although the independent detectors
reveal a subtle brow entanglement. This is a feature and a
limitation at once: the rendered change is a natural, coherent expression, but AU12 is not isolable. It is the empirical basis for the multi-AU extension proposed in
Chapter~\ref{cap:schedule}.

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/2026-08-25-au-coactivation-profile.pdf}
    \caption[Whole-face response to the AU12 ramp.]{Per-action-unit response to the AU12 ramp
    (Spearman $r$, trained adaptor versus frozen baseline). The smile cluster (AU12, AU6, AU25)
    co-activates and the mouth-closing units (AU23, AU17) anti-correlate, while brow units are
    near zero. Asterisks mark units significantly modulated by training ($q<0.05$,
    Benjamini--Hochberg), and units drawn in grey were detected in fewer than half of the clips

\section{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:res-threats}
\todo[inline]{Review if this section is necessary}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Label--evaluation circularity.} py-feat provides both the training labels and the primary metric. This is already mitigated by the independent MediaPipe measurement in Section~\ref{subsec:eval-circularity}, but not eliminated.
    \item \textbf{Demographic bias.} Ramp $r$ is $0.83$ for female prompts against $0.71$ for male prompts. FineFace shows the opposite skew ($0.82$ male against $0.77$ female), which localises the cause to the \gls{mead} training distribution rather than to AU-conditioned generation in general.
    \item \textbf{Lab-domain training data.} A single dataset, a single emotion category, frontal views, controlled lighting. Generalisation to in-the-wild portraits is untested.
    \item \textbf{Single control axis.} Only AU12 is conditioned. The whole-face analysis shows the axis co-activates the entire smile cluster and cannot isolate a single unit; the prior art controls twelve action units and their combinations. A multi-AU axis is proposed in Chapter~\ref{cap:schedule}.
\end{itemize}
